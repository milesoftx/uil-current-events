T01 · Texas · Government and rights

Congressional redistricting

August 2025 to 2026

What happened: Texas Republicans redrew U.S. House districts between census years. Democratic lawmakers left the state to deny the House a quorum and delay action. Gov. Greg Abbott signed the map on August 29, 2025. It was designed to give Republicans five additional favorable seats. On December 4, the U.S. Supreme Court allowed the map to be used while litigation continued.

Why it matters: District boundaries can change which voters are grouped together and which party is likely to win. A favorable district is an opportunity, not a guaranteed election victory.

Know these: Greg Abbott; Texas Legislature; quorum; redistricting; gerrymandering. Texas has 38 U.S. House seats.

Think it through: How does changing a district's boundaries affect representation without changing the number of seats?

Interest rates and the cost of living

September 2025 to October 2026

What happened: On September 17, 2025, the Federal Reserve cut its benchmark target range by one-quarter percentage point to 4.00-4.25%, citing changing economic risks. On October 14, 2026, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that September consumer prices were 3.7% higher than a year earlier and 0.6% higher than the previous month.

Update, October 18: The September 16, 2026 Federal Reserve decision raised the target range to 3.75–4.00%, superseding the historical September 2025 range above. The October 14 BLS release showed gasoline prices rose 9.1% in September, accounting for more than half of that month’s CPI increase. Future rate decisions remained undecided; forecasts are not policy actions.

Why it matters: Rate decisions balance inflation and employment. Slower inflation means prices rise more slowly; it does not necessarily mean prices fall. The 2025 rate is a historical figure, not a statement of the current rate.

Know these: Federal Reserve; FOMC; federal funds rate; BLS; CPI; inflation; purchasing power.

Think it through: Why might policies intended to reduce inflation also make borrowing and hiring more difficult?

N15 · United States · Government and rights

Midterm voting disputes and No Kings rallies

October 15–25, 2026

What happened: On October 15, the Justice Department sued California, seeking to set aside about 20,000 mail ballots from voters it suspected might be noncitizens until eligibility checks were completed. California challenged the list’s accuracy. On October 17, the No Kings network held hundreds of rallies encouraging early voting; organizers called them nonpartisan, while the movement grew from opposition to President Trump. On October 19, federal prosecutors separately charged 28 lawful permanent residents with registration or voting offenses; charges are allegations, not convictions. On October 22, the Supreme Court granted an emergency appeal allowing New Hampshire to bar student IDs as either primary or backup voter identification for the midterms. On October 25, a federal appeals panel questioned the Justice Department’s California ballot request and had not ruled by this cutoff.

Why it matters: The disputes connect election administration, federal and state authority, evidence standards, and the risk of wrongly burdening eligible voters. An emergency court order can set rules for an election without resolving every underlying legal question. Demonstration attendance and early-vote totals do not predict an election winner.

Know these: Department of Justice; California; New Hampshire; mail ballot; voter identification; lawful permanent resident; due process; emergency appeal; midterm election. Noncitizens cannot vote in federal elections.

Think it through: What evidence and review process should officials require before questioning a voter or setting aside a ballot? How should courts weigh legal claims when Election Day is near?

Ukraine diplomacy and continuing war

August 2025 to October 2026

What happened: Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin met in Alaska on August 15, 2025, to discuss Russia's war in Ukraine. The summit did not produce a deal ending the war. Fighting continued into 2026, including attacks on civilian areas and infrastructure.

Update, October 18: On October 16, Trump issued an order waiving new Russia-related sanctions, drawing criticism from lawmakers in both parties. The waiver followed his Russian diesel-import initiative aimed at energy prices; it did not establish peace. Separately, Reuters reported on October 16 that Ukrainian troops experienced disrupted Starlink communications. Officials cited by Reuters attributed an order restricting Starshield, its military service, to the White House; a U.S. official denied that Trump ordered a deliberate slowdown. Zelenskyy said service had improved but was not fully restored that day. Treat responsibility claims as attributed reporting.

Update, October 25: SpaceX restored full-speed Starshield service after an earlier disruption, but Ukrainian officials and Elon Musk publicly disputed why restrictions had been imposed and whether military use violated Starlink terms. The U.S. and Ukrainian governments also disputed who controlled the military-service changes. Fighting continued: President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Russia launched nearly 2,300 strike drones, 48 missiles, and about 1,600 guided bombs during the week; local authorities reported four deaths in overnight attacks on the Dnipropetrovsk region. These figures and responsibility claims are attributed to the officials who made them.

Why it matters: Negotiations involve territory, sovereignty, security guarantees, sanctions, and support for Ukraine. Holding a summit does not establish that a ceasefire or peace agreement exists.

Know these: Russia: Vladimir Putin; Ukraine: Volodymyr Zelenskyy; Alaska summit; NATO; sanctions; sovereignty.

October 25, 2026

What happened: Brazil’s electoral court confirmed that President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won reelection to a fourth nonconsecutive term. With 100% counted, Lula received 51.8% and led Sen. The result affects Brazil’s approach to the Amazon, social policy, and relations with the United States. A presidential victory does not guarantee control of Congress, where right-wing parties made gains.

Key terms

Antitrust
Rules intended to protect competition and prohibit certain anticompetitive conduct.
Appropriation
Legal authorization to spend government money for specified purposes.
CPI
Consumer Price Index, a measure of price changes for a basket of consumer goods and services.
Ceasefire
An agreement or arrangement to stop fighting; it need not settle the underlying conflict.
Coalition
Parties or groups that cooperate to form a government or pursue a shared goal.
Contact tracing
Identifying and following people who may have been exposed to an infectious disease.
Continuing resolution
A law providing temporary funding when regular appropriations are unfinished.
Deductible
An amount a person pays for covered health services before certain insurance payments begin.
Due process
Fair legal procedures before government deprives someone of protected rights or interests.
Eminent domain
Government power to acquire private property for public use, with required compensation.
Export control
A restriction on selling or transferring selected goods or technologies abroad.
Federalism
Division of authority between a national government and regional governments, such as states or provinces.
Gerrymandering
Drawing political districts to advantage a party or group.
Homestead exemption
An amount excluded from a qualifying home's taxable value.
Humanitarian access
The ability of relief workers and supplies to reach people who need assistance safely and consistently.
Inflation
An increase in the general price level over time.
Injunction
A court order requiring or stopping an action; some injunctions are temporary.
Mitigation and adaptation
Climate mitigation reduces emissions; adaptation reduces vulnerability to climate impacts.
Premium
The recurring payment needed to maintain an insurance policy.
Provisional ballot
A ballot kept separate until officials verify that the voter is eligible and the ballot meets applicable rules.
Quorum
The minimum number of members required for a legislative body to conduct specified business.
Redaction
Removal or obscuring of information in a record before release.
Redistricting
Redrawing electoral boundaries; different from reapportionment, which redistributes seats.
Referendum
A direct vote on a policy or constitutional question; its legal effect depends on the rules governing it.
Sanction
In foreign policy, a restriction intended to pressure a government, organization, or person.
Secession
The withdrawal of a region from an existing country or political union.
Sovereignty
A state's authority over its territory and political affairs.
Storm surge
An abnormal rise in seawater associated with a storm, beyond normal predicted tides.
Subsidy
Government assistance that reduces costs or supports an activity.
Tariff
A tax on imported goods, collected from the importer.
Vote dilution
Reducing a group's practical ability to elect representatives of its choice.
Waiver
An authorized exception to a requirement; a sanctions waiver does not necessarily repeal the underlying law.
Runoff election
A second election held when no candidate met the rule needed to win the first round.
